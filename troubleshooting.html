Why transfers peel, crack or fade

Almost every DTF failure comes down to one of three things: not enough pressure, the wrong temperature, or moisture where it should not be. This is how to work backwards from what you are looking at to which one it was.

Diagnose from the symptom

Find what you are seeing in the left column. The cause is usually the first one listed — they are ordered by how often they turn out to be the answer.

Edges lift after a wash or two Not enough pressure, or heat did not reach the adhesive Increase pressure firmly; add 3–5 seconds; check the press is even across the platen

Cracks when the garment is stretched Over-heated, or an adhesive not rated for stretch Lower the temperature; use a stretch-capable powder on lycra and sportswear
Cracks with no stretching, straight away Too much heat — the ink has become brittle Drop 10–15 °C and lengthen the press slightly

Pressure is underrated

If a transfer lifts, the instinct is to turn up the heat. Usually the problem was pressure. The adhesive has to be physically pushed into the weave of the fabric while it is molten — heat alone just melts it on the surface, where it sits until the first wash takes it off.

Proper DTF pressure is firm. On a clamshell press you should have to lean on the handle to close it, not just swing it shut. If it closes easily, it is too light.

With a household iron, pressure is the hard part — an iron weighs very little on its own. Press down with real force, work in sections, and make sure the surface underneath is hard. An ironing board absorbs pressure and is one reason home application fails.

Quick check

Press a test piece, and after peeling look at the fabric around the design. You should see a faint impression of the transfer's edge pushed into the weave. If the surface looks untouched, you did not use enough pressure.

Hot peel and cold peel are not interchangeable

Films are made for one or the other, and getting it wrong is the most common reason a design comes away attached to the film.

  • Hot peel — remove immediately while still hot. Waiting lets the adhesive set to the film.
  • Warm peel — wait a few seconds until it is hand-warm.
  • Cold peel — wait until fully cool. Peeling early takes the design with it.

If you do not know which yours is, ask the supplier rather than guessing. A cold-peel film peeled hot will fail every time and looks exactly like a bonding problem.

Too much heat causes more problems than too little

Under-heating gives you a weak bond, which shows up straight away and is easy to correct. Over-heating causes several different failures that are easy to misdiagnose:

  • Ink goes brittle and cracks
  • Colours dull and shift
  • Polyester dye migrates into the print
  • The garment scorches or takes a permanent shine
  • Synthetic fabric glazes or melts

When something is wrong and you are not sure what, try reducing temperature and increasing time before doing the opposite. It fixes more than it breaks.

Aftercare that actually matters

A well-pressed transfer will outlast the garment if it is looked after. Most of the wear complaints trace back to the first wash.

  • Wait 24 hours before the first wash. The bond keeps curing after it cools.
  • Turn inside out so the print is not abraded against the drum.
  • Cold wash, gentle cycle.
  • No bleach, no fabric softener. Softener coats fibres and works its way under print edges.
  • Air dry. Tumble drying is heat plus abrasion, which is precisely the combination that lifts edges.
  • Never iron directly on the design. Iron inside out if needed.

Passing these instructions to customers is worth doing. A large share of "your print failed" complaints are a hot wash and a tumble dry.

When it is the artwork, not the press

Some failures are baked in before the film is printed, and no press setting rescues them:

  • A white box around the design — the file had no transparent background. Nothing at the press fixes this.
  • Blurry or blocky edges — the artwork was too low-resolution for the printed size.
  • Thin details disappearing — hairlines below roughly 1 mm at printed size struggle to hold, especially through a wash cycle.
  • A pale halo on dark garments — the white underbase showing at the edges, usually from very fine detail.

The artwork preparation guide covers how to catch these before printing — and the nester flags low-resolution and non-transparent files automatically when you load them.

Common questions

My design came off with the film. What went wrong?

Nearly always the peel timing. Check whether your film is hot, warm or cold peel and follow it precisely. If timing was right, the next suspects are insufficient pressure and not enough time under heat.

How long should a DTF print last?

Pressed properly and washed cold, it should comfortably outlast the garment — dozens of washes without visible change. A print failing within a handful of washes points to a pressing fault, not a material limit.

Can I re-press a transfer that is lifting?

Often yes, if it is caught early. Cover with parchment and press again at the correct settings with firm pressure. It works best when the cause was too little pressure or time; it will not rescue a print already cracked from over-heating.

Why is there a shiny square around my design?

The press platen has glazed the fabric — most common on polyester and dark colours. Use a cover sheet larger than the platen, and lower the temperature slightly. It sometimes relaxes after a wash, but prevention is easier.

Is a heat press really better than an iron?

For consistency, yes — it gives even pressure across the whole design and a known temperature. An iron works fine for one-offs and small runs if you press hard on a hard surface. For production volumes, uneven iron pressure becomes the main source of failures.
