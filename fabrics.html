Which fabrics DTF works on

The big advantage of direct-to-film over most other decoration methods is that it does not much care what the garment is made of. It still cares about heat, though, and one fabric in particular will ruin a job days after it looked perfect.

Why DTF is so forgiving

Screen printing needs ink chemistry matched to the fabric. Sublimation only works on polyester, and only on light colours. DTF sidesteps both problems: the design is printed onto film, coated with a hot-melt adhesive powder, and that adhesive is what bonds to the garment.

Because the bond is mechanical adhesive rather than dye chemistry, the same transfer works on a white cotton tee and a black polyester hoodie. What changes between fabrics is not whether it works, but how much heat the fabric can take.

Fabric compatibility at a glance

Dye migration: the one that bites late

This is the single most expensive fabric problem in garment decoration, because you do not see it at the time. Everything looks perfect when you peel the film. A day or two later, the white parts of the design have turned pink.

The cause is the polyester itself. Polyester is coloured with disperse dye, and disperse dye turns to gas when heated. Under a heat press that gas moves upward into your transfer and stains it from behind. Red, maroon and hot pink polyester are the worst offenders, followed by navy and purple.

How to avoid it

  • Press cooler and longer. Dye migration is driven by temperature far more than by time. Dropping 15–20 °C and adding a few seconds is the main defence.
  • Use a blocking or barrier film if your supplier offers one for polyester.
  • Let it cool before stacking. Garments stacked hot keep cooking each other, and migration continues in the pile.
  • Test one garment and wait 48 hours before running a large order in dark polyester. That wait is the only real test.

Worth telling customers

If a customer supplies their own red or maroon polyester garments, warn them about migration before you print. It is a property of their fabric, not a fault in the transfer, but you will be the one blamed if nobody mentioned it.

Always pre-press the garment

Two to five seconds under the press before the transfer goes on does three useful things: it drives out moisture, it flattens the fibres, and it removes creases that would otherwise become permanent lines under the design.

Moisture matters more than people expect. Cotton straight off a shelf holds enough humidity to turn to steam under the press, and steam between the adhesive and the fabric is exactly where a transfer fails to bond. In a humid climate this is not optional.

Test before a long run

A fabric that behaves in the press can still fail in the wash. Before committing to a large order on an unfamiliar garment, print one, press it, and wash it a few times.

  1. Press a single test piece at your intended settings.
  2. Let it rest 24 hours — bonds continue to set after cooling.
  3. Wash cold, inside out, and air dry.
  4. Check the edges first: lifting starts at corners and thin details.
  5. Stretch it firmly. Cracking under stretch means the adhesive is wrong for that fabric.

On dark polyester, add the 48-hour wait for migration before deciding it passed.

Garment colour and the white underbase

DTF lays white ink beneath the colours, which is why the same transfer looks right on both a white and a black shirt. That white layer is generated from the transparency in your artwork, which is why a file with no transparent background prints a white rectangle.

There is a practical consequence: very fine details and thin lines carry the same white underbase, and on very dark garments the slightest misalignment shows as a pale halo. If a design has hairline elements, thickening them slightly is worth doing.

Common questions

Can I use the same transfer on cotton and polyester?

Yes — the transfer itself is the same. What changes is the press setting: polyester wants noticeably lower heat, compensated with a little more time.

Why did my white print turn pink after a day?

Dye migration from the polyester underneath. The garment's dye has gassed under heat and stained the transfer from behind. Press cooler next time, and let garments cool flat rather than stacking them warm.

Will DTF work on a waterproof jacket?

Usually not. The coating that makes it waterproof also stops adhesive bonding, and most shell fabrics cannot take press temperatures. Always test a hidden area or a spare first.

Does it work on towels or fleece blankets?

Fleece is fine if you pre-press to flatten the pile. Towelling is not — there is no flat surface for the adhesive to grip, so the edges lift quickly.

What about stretchy sportswear?

It works, but the adhesive has to be able to stretch with the fabric. A standard powder on lycra will craze the first time it is pulled on. Ask your supplier for a stretch-capable powder and test it by stretching a sample hard.
