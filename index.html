What a gang sheet actually is

Direct-to-film printing runs on a continuous roll, usually 22 or 24 inches wide, and the printer bills by the length it pulls through. A gang sheet is simply many separate designs arranged onto one length of that film so they print in a single pass.

The width is fixed by the machine. The only thing you control is how much length a job consumes — and that is decided entirely by how tightly the designs are packed. Two shops printing the same forty stickers can easily differ by a third in film used, and the one packing loosely is quietly burning the difference on every order.

Traditionally someone arranges this by hand in CorelDRAW or Photoshop. It is slow, it is repetitive, and a human eye is not good at the geometry. Fitting rectangles into a fixed-width strip with minimum waste is a well-studied computing problem, and a machine is simply better at it.

Rule of thumb

A hand-built sheet typically lands somewhere around 60–70% covered. Anything below that and you are paying for a lot of nothing. Automatic nesting should be getting you into the 80s on a mixed job.

Preparing artwork: the four things that matter

Most problems on a DTF sheet trace back to the file, not the printer. These four catch nearly all of them.

1. The background must be genuinely transparent

DTF lays a white ink underbase wherever your artwork has pixels, then colour on top. The printer decides where to put that white from the file's alpha channel. If your file has no transparency — a JPEG, or a PNG flattened onto white — the machine sees a solid rectangle and prints one: your logo arrives on a hard-edged white card.

Save as PNG with a transparent background. If you are handed a JPEG, the background has to be cut out before it is any use. There is no setting on the printer that fixes this afterwards.

2. Resolution is measured at the printed size, not in pixels

"It's a big file" means nothing on its own. What matters is pixels divided by printed inches. A 600 × 600 px logo is fine at 2 inches and poor at 6, because the same pixels are being stretched across three times the fabric.

Effective resolutionResult on fabricVerdict
300 DPISharp edges, clean small textIdeal
200–299 DPISharp to the eye at arm's lengthFine
150–199 DPISlightly soft edges; acceptable on large artWorkable
100–149 DPIVisibly soft, fine detail mushyRisky
Below 100 DPIBlocky, jagged edges, unusable textDo not print

The arithmetic is simple: pixels ÷ inches = DPI. A 1200 px wide file printed 4 inches across gives 300 DPI. The same file at 6 inches gives 200. At 12 inches it is 100 and it will look it.

Enlarging a small file does not add detail — it only spreads the pixels you already had. If the number comes out low, the answer is a bigger original or a smaller print, never upscaling.

3. Trim the empty space around the design

This is the one that quietly costs the most. Artwork routinely arrives as a small design floating in the middle of an enormous transparent canvas. Laid out by eye that padding looks like nothing; laid out by machine it is treated as part of the design, and the surrounding emptiness gets reserved as if it were ink.

Crop every file to the actual edges of the artwork before nesting. On a busy sheet this alone can be the difference between one length of film and two.

4. Know whether your RIP mirrors

A DTF transfer is printed onto film, dusted with adhesive powder, cured, then pressed face down onto the garment. The image therefore has to be laid down mirrored so it reads correctly once flipped over.

Most RIP software does this for you. Some does not. It is worth knowing which yours is before you find out on a run of fifty shirts with backwards lettering — anything with text is where you notice.

How sizes work when artwork is not square

Transfers are usually sold in fixed size tiers — 2 × 2, 4 × 4, A4 and so on. That raises an obvious question: what does "4 × 4" mean for a design that is long and thin?

The sane answer, and the one used here, is that a size is a box the artwork fits inside. Nothing is stretched and nothing is cropped. The longest side of the design grows until it reaches the size ordered, and the other side follows the original proportions.

Artwork shapeOrdered as 2 × 2Ordered as 4 × 4
Square2 × 2 in4 × 4 in
Wide banner (3:1)2 × 0.67 in4 × 1.33 in
Tall design (1:5)0.4 × 2 in0.8 × 4 in
Photo (4:3)2 × 1.5 in4 × 3 in

Paper sizes such as A5, A4 and A3 are not square, so they turn to match the artwork. A landscape design ordered as A4 should use the full 11.7 inch side rather than being held to 8.3 — otherwise wide designs quietly get shortchanged against square ones at the same price.

Gaps, margins and the edge of the roll

Designs need a little space between them so they can be cut apart without clipping a neighbour. Around 3–4 mm (about ⅛ inch) is the usual working figure. It is tempting to shrink this to save film, but a gap too tight costs far more in ruined transfers than it saves in length.

Leave a margin at the edges of the roll too — roughly 5 mm (¼ inch). Film feed is never perfectly straight over a long run, and artwork pressed right against the edge is the first thing to suffer.

Worth knowing

A Photoshop PSD cannot exceed 30,000 pixels on a side, which at 300 DPI is exactly 100 inches. Longer runs have to be split into multiple sheets, or printed at a lower resolution. This is a limit of the file format, not of your printer.

Applying a transfer at home

DTF transfers do not strictly need a heat press. A domestic iron works, provided the pressure is firm and even.

  1. Place the garment on a flat, hard surface — not a padded ironing board — and smooth out the wrinkles.
  2. Position the transfer with the printed side facing up.
  3. Cover it with a thin cloth or baking parchment.
  4. Heat the iron to its cotton setting, with steam off.
  5. Press firmly with constant pressure for 15–20 seconds without sliding the iron around.
  6. Peel after letting it cool for a few seconds, pulling the film back gently.

Afterwards: turn the garment inside out before washing, use a cold machine wash, and skip bleach and the tumble dryer. Never iron directly onto the design itself. Give it 24 hours before the first wash.

Common questions

Why does my design have a white box around it?

The file has no transparent background — almost always because it is a JPEG, or a PNG that was flattened. DTF prints white ink wherever the file says there are pixels, so a solid background becomes a solid printed rectangle. The background has to be removed in the artwork before printing.

Can you enlarge my small logo to A3?

It can be enlarged, but no detail is created by doing so — the existing pixels are just spread further apart, and the result looks soft or blocky. Check pixels ÷ inches: below about 150 you will see it on the garment. A vector file or a larger original is the real fix.

Should I send CMYK or RGB files?

Send RGB. The RIP converts to the printer's own ink set and generates the white underbase from the transparency. Converting to CMYK yourself first usually dulls the colours without helping anything.

Do I need to mirror the artwork before sending it?

Normally no — mirroring is the printer's job, and most RIP software does it automatically. Send the artwork the right way round unless you have specifically been asked otherwise, or you risk it being mirrored twice.

Why is my gang sheet longer than I expected?

Usually transparent padding around the designs, which reserves space as though it were artwork. Trimming each file to the edge of its design is the single biggest saving available. Awkward shapes and a large gap setting account for most of the rest.

Can several customers' orders share one sheet?

Yes, and it is normally the cheapest way to print. The practical difficulty is sorting afterwards, so name the layers or keep a job sheet listing what belongs to whom before the film is cut.
